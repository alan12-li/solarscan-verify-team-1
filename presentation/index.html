SolarScan Verify

A generative-AI verification layer for Con Edison's rooftop solar scanner — turning ambiguous roofs into solar / no solar / uncertain, with humans in the loop.

Problem slice

The hard cases: HVAC, skylights, shadows, obstructions

The problem: the scanner is right until it isn't

Con Edison's existing rooftop solar scanner handles straightforward roofs well. The failures concentrate on complex New York City rooftops — HVAC units, skylights, shadows, obstructions, unusual layouts — where equipment gets mistaken for panels, or real panels get missed.

Costly error A

False "solar" → field team visits a roof with no panels.

Costly error B

False "no solar" → real generation potential is missed.

Current state

Ambiguous cases are reviewed by hand — imagery, records, judgment.

Why this slice

We deliberately chose one part of the problem: the low-confidence verification step. Not a new scanner, not streaming imagery — a review layer for the cases the existing system cannot settle.

The approach: a verification layer, not a replacement

  1. Same inputs, several models. Four vision models — Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite (baseline), GPT-5.5, Kimi K3, Gemma 4 26B — classify each roof under one output contract.
  2. Structured output. label + confidence + reason + difficulty factors + escalate flag (PRD §3 contract).
  3. Fail toward a person. uncertain or low-confidence ⇒ escalate to an analyst. The system recommends; a person decides.

Benchmark

30 rooftop images (19 test + 11 valid) from a CC-licensed public drone dataset. Ground truth = majority vote of 3 labelers (14 solar / 16 no_solar). Image integrity verified by SHA-256 before labeling.

Most errors are "too honest", not "too bold"

The dominant failure is no_solar → uncertain (24 of 37 total errors): models hedge on roofs humans call clearly no-solar. That direction is recoverable — the verification layer routes them to a person.

But confident reverse calls exist

no_solar → solar appeared 3 times (Gemini 1, Kimi 2) — the costly direction that would send a field team to a roof with no panels. This is the error class a verification layer must catch.

Confidence is informative

Models that answered correctly averaged 0.78–0.96 confidence; models that erred averaged 0.39–0.47. Low confidence correlates with wrong answers — a usable escalation signal.

Baseline case: everyone passes

When models agree, all four hit 83% — the scanner's easy work is genuinely easier. This is the case the existing scanner already handles.

Harder case: the verification layer's territory

On the 18 roofs where models disagree, GPT-5.5 holds 83% but Gemini drops to 67% and Gemma collapses to 33%. These are exactly the roofs a verification layer must escalate to a person.

Surprising fact from the data

Humans hesitated — models didn't

On the 5 rooftops where our first two labelers disagreed, the models still output confident solar/no_solar calls. Example sv-0133: two humans said no solar, yet Kimi K3 called it solar — a model confident the opposite of a human consensus. A third labeler later settled each dispute 2:1.

Open-weights models are honest about doubt

Gemma 4 26B marked the most roofs uncertain (11/30) and Kimi K3 (8/30) was above both closed models. The cheapest model (Gemma, 10× cheaper than GPT-5.5) is the least over-confident — the safer failure direction for a verification layer.

Why this matters

A scanner that is confident on exactly the roofs humans hesitate over is the failure mode a verification layer must catch.

One approach that failed — and why

Failed: bare-prompt classification with the default setup

  • What we tried: first pass with Gemini 2.5 Flash models, plain "classify this roof" prompt, default parsing.
  • What failed: 2.5 models returned 404 for new accounts; one model emitted malformed/multi-object JSON; free-tier rate limits (429) stalled ~40 calls.
  • Evidence: result files with error rows; the retry/resume logic in evaluate_benchmark.py; the robust JSON extractor added after the parse failures.
  • Lesson: treat every model output as a candidate, verify the pipeline before trusting any number.

Second failure, worth naming: escalation recall

The scorecard itself caught it — models output confident solar/no_solar on roofs both human labelers called uncertain. A confident wrong answer on an ambiguous roof is exactly what a verification layer must prevent.

One approach that looks promising

Promising: structured three-way contract + human escalation

  • The PRD output contract works: all four models produced parseable label/confidence/reason JSON across 120 calls after the parser fix — the schema is enforceable.
  • Agreement on solar is real: all models broadly agree on solar cases — clear cases are separable.
  • The escalation path is the differentiator: a verification layer that routes uncertain/low-confidence cases to a person catches the 60% of roofs where models disagree.
  • Next test: build a calibration set of genuinely ambiguous roofs (where labelers tie) and measure whether prompt tuning moves escalation recall toward 1.0.

Recommendation: revise, then a limited test

What the evidence supports now

  • Not "stop", not "deploy as-is". Baseline-case accuracy is 83% but drops to 33% (Gemma) on the harder roofs — the models cannot yet be trusted on the roofs that matter most.
  • Revise the escalation behavior first (prompt / threshold), then run a limited test: 100 roofs, measure escalation recall toward 1.0 and how many human reviews are avoided.
  • Cost is not the blocker. Per correct clear-case: $0.003 (Gemini), $0.013 (GPT-5.5), $0.002 (Gemma) — even the best model costs cents per roof.

The value we're after

Today an ambiguous roof means a manual review. A verification layer with a trustworthy escalation path could cut manual reviews from roughly 50 to ~17 per 100 roofs (scanner's low-confidence cases → our escalation rate) while keeping a person on every genuinely hard call.

What could go wrong

  • Escalation recall stays low → the layer rubber-stamps wrong answers (the failure we already measured).
  • Escalation rate explodes → no manual-review savings; the layer adds noise.
  • Thermal/imagery quality differs from our public dataset → NYC field imagery needs its own calibration set.

What we'd ask Con Edison

  • Which error has historically cost more — false solar or false no-solar?
  • What context data (footprints, permits, historical imagery) is shareable?
  • How should results return to your review tooling?

SolarScan Verify: the scanner stays; the ambiguous roofs get a second, explainable, human-escalated look.
